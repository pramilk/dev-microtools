faq:
  - q: Is my PDF uploaded to a server?
    a: No. The PDF is read and its images are decoded entirely in your browser, in a background worker so the page stays responsive. Nothing you choose is transmitted anywhere — you can disconnect from the internet after the page loads and the tool still works.
  - q: Are the images extracted at their original quality?
    a: Yes. JPEG images are saved exactly as they are stored inside the PDF, byte for byte, so there is no re-compression at all. Images stored as raw or Flate-compressed pixels are decoded and saved as lossless PNG, so not a single pixel changes. You get each image at the resolution it was embedded at, which is often higher than it appears on the page.
  - q: Why are some images PNG when I expected JPEG?
    a: A PDF has no PNG format of its own — a PNG placed in a document is stored as raw, deflate-compressed pixels, and the same goes for screenshots and most diagrams. Those are saved as PNG because it is the lossless format that matches. A JPEG with a transparency mask is also saved as PNG, since JPEG can't hold transparency. If you need a different format, the Image Format Converter can turn the files into JPG, WebP or AVIF.
  - q: Why does an image look different from the page, or why is something missing?
    a: The tool extracts each image as it is stored. A page can crop, rotate, recolour or overlap an image when drawing it, and those page-level effects aren't part of the image itself. Text, lines and shapes drawn with vector graphics aren't images at all, so they don't appear — to capture a whole page as a picture, use a PDF-to-image renderer instead. A few image types can't be decoded in a browser (JBIG2 and CCITT black-and-white scans), and the results list them so nothing is skipped silently. Small inline images embedded directly in a page's drawing instructions aren't extracted either.
  - q: What happens to CMYK images?
    a: CMYK JPEGs are saved as stored, and image editors and viewers open them normally. Uncompressed CMYK images are converted to RGB for the PNG with a simple formula, so colours can look slightly more saturated than in a colour-managed print workflow. JPEG 2000 images are saved as .jp2 files, which most image editors open, but which browsers other than Safari can't preview.
  - q: Can it extract images from a password-protected PDF?
    a: No — an encrypted PDF's image data can't be read without its password, and browser PDF libraries can't decrypt it. Open the file in a PDF viewer, use "Print to PDF" to save an unprotected copy, and extract from that instead.

## Extracting images vs. converting pages to images

There are two different ways to get pictures out of a PDF, and they suit different jobs.
**Extracting**, which is what this tool does, saves the image files the document actually
contains: the original photos, logos and charts, at their own resolution and in their own
format, without any of the text around them. **Rendering** a page instead takes a snapshot
of the whole page as it appears on screen, text and all. If you want the photo from page 3
to reuse elsewhere, extract it. If you want a picture of page 3, render it.

A scanned document is the case where the two meet: each page of a scan is usually a single
full-page image, so extracting gives you the scanner's original page images at their
original resolution, which is ideal for OCR.

## What the gallery tells you

Each card lists the image's **pixel size**, its **format** (JPEG for images stored as JPEG,
PNG for everything else), its file size, the **pages** it appears on, and how its colour was
stored (RGB, grayscale, CMYK, palette or 1-bit). Images that carry transparency are shown on
a checkerboard so you can see what's see-through. A logo that's embedded as several identical
copies (merge tools often do this) is shown once, with all of its pages listed.

## What powers this tool

PDF parsing uses [`pdf-lib`](https://github.com/Hopding/pdf-lib) (MIT licensed), with this
site's own decoder for PDF pixel formats, predictors, palettes and masks. PNG encoding uses
your browser's built-in image codecs, and the ZIP is built with
[`fflate`](https://github.com/101arrowz/fflate) (MIT licensed). All of it runs locally on
your device.
