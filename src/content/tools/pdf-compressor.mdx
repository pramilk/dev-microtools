faq:
  - q: Is my PDF uploaded to a server?
    a: No. The PDF is read, compressed and rebuilt entirely in your browser, in a background worker so the page stays responsive. Nothing you choose is transmitted anywhere — you can disconnect from the internet after the page loads and the tool still works.
  - q: Why can't I choose WebP, AVIF or PNG as the image format?
    a: A PDF can only store images in a few specific encodings — JPEG, JPEG 2000, and raw pixels compressed with Flate (the same deflate algorithm used inside PNG files). WebP and AVIF images simply aren't allowed inside a PDF, and a PDF reader couldn't display them. The Lossless option here is the PDF equivalent of PNG, using the same row filters and compression a PNG file uses internally. JPEG 2000 isn't offered because browsers can't encode it.
  - q: Which settings give the smallest file?
    a: Resolution is usually the biggest lever — a 300 DPI scan capped at 150 DPI has a quarter of the pixels. After that, a lower JPEG quality and grayscale conversion help most. The Strong preset combines 96 DPI with 50% JPEG quality. For documents with mostly text and few images, expect modest savings — text and vector graphics are already compact, and this tool deliberately never rasterizes them.
  - q: Will the text still be searchable and selectable?
    a: Yes. Only embedded images are re-encoded; text, fonts and vector drawings are kept exactly as they were (their streams are only losslessly recompressed). Some online compressors "print" every page into an image, which makes a smaller file but destroys text selection, search and accessibility — this tool never does that.
  - q: Why did my PDF barely shrink, or not shrink at all?
    a: Either the PDF is mostly text and vectors, which are already compact, or its images are in formats this tool leaves untouched — JPEG 2000, JBIG2 and CCITT fax scans (the last two are already extremely efficient for black-and-white pages), or CMYK print images, whose colours could shift if re-encoded in a browser. The results panel lists exactly which images were skipped and why. If the rewritten file wouldn't be smaller, the tool tells you instead of handing back a larger "compressed" file.
  - q: Can it compress a password-protected PDF?
    a: No — an encrypted PDF can't be rewritten without its password, and browser PDF libraries can't re-encrypt the result. Open the file in a PDF viewer, use "Print to PDF" to save an unprotected copy, and compress that instead.

## Choosing format, quality and resolution

**JPEG** gives the smallest files and is the right choice for photos and scanned pages.
**Lossless** never changes a pixel, so it only saves space through downsampling and
grayscale conversion — use it when an image contains fine text or line art that must stay
crisp. **Match original** is the careful middle ground: images that were already JPEG are
re-encoded at your chosen quality, and lossless images (screenshots, diagrams) stay
lossless. Images that carry transparency are always kept lossless whatever you choose,
since JPEG artefacts in a transparency mask show up as halos around edges.

The resolution cap is measured against the document's largest page: at 150 DPI an image
drawn across a full Letter or A4 page keeps about 1650 pixels on its long side. That's
sharp on screen and fine for office printing; choose 300 DPI if the PDF is headed to a
professional printer.

## What powers this tool

PDF parsing and writing use [`pdf-lib`](https://github.com/Hopding/pdf-lib) (MIT
licensed). Stream compression uses [`fflate`](https://github.com/101arrowz/fflate) (MIT
licensed). JPEG decoding and encoding use your browser's own built-in image codecs. All of
it runs locally on your device.
