whatIsIt: >-
  An ISO 8601 duration is a compact, language-neutral way to write a length of time:
  `P3Y6M4DT12H30M5S` means 3 years, 6 months, 4 days, 12 hours, 30 minutes and 5 seconds. The P
  stands for period, the T separates the date units from the time units, and each number is
  followed by a letter naming its unit. Durations show up in YouTube and media APIs, calendar
  and schedule files, OpenAPI and JSON Schema, Java's `Duration`, .NET's `XmlConvert`, and
  anywhere a timeout or an interval has to be stored as text. This tool reads one and explains
  it in plain English with totals in seconds, minutes, hours and days, can apply it to a start
  date to show where it lands, and builds a correctly formed string from separate fields.

faq:
  - q: What does the T mean, and why is M used twice?
    a: The T separates the date part from the time part. M means months before the T and minutes after it, so P1M is one month and PT1M is one minute. That is the single most common source of bugs with these strings, which is why the parser reads the letters by position and shows the result in words so you can check it.
  - q: Are weeks allowed together with days?
    a: The original 2004 standard only allowed weeks on their own, as in P2W. The 2019 edition and most libraries accept them alongside other units, so the parser accepts P1W2D but shows a warning, because a stricter parser elsewhere in your stack may reject it.
  - q: Can a duration have decimals?
    a: Yes, but only on the smallest unit you use - PT1.5H or PT0.5S are valid, while P1.5DT2H is not, because it would be ambiguous. Both a dot and a comma are accepted as the decimal mark. Fractional years and months are accepted by the parser but cannot be added to a calendar date, since the length of a year or month depends on which one you mean; express the fraction in days instead.
  - q: Why are the totals marked approximate?
    a: A month can be 28, 29, 30 or 31 days and a year 365 or 366, so "one month" has no fixed number of seconds. The totals use the average Gregorian values - 365.2425 days a year and a twelfth of that for a month - and say so. If you apply the duration to a start date, the answer is exact for that date.
  - q: What about negative durations?
    a: A leading minus, as in -P1D, is not part of the original standard but is widely used (Java, the 2019 edition of ISO 8601, and many APIs). The parser accepts it and applies it as subtraction when you give it a start date.

## Reading a duration at a glance

Split at the T. Everything before it is calendar units - years, months, weeks, days - and
everything after is clock units - hours, minutes, seconds. `P1DT12H` is a day and a half.
`PT90M` is the same as `PT1H30M`; durations are not required to be normalised, and this tool
shows both the string you gave and its canonical form.

## Why not just store seconds?

A number of seconds is exact but loses intent. "One month" and "30 days" are different promises
on a billing system, and "one day" is not always 86,400 seconds across a daylight saving change.
An ISO 8601 duration keeps the unit the author meant. When you do need a plain number, for a
timeout or a sleep, the totals in this tool give it to you, and the seconds-to-duration option
in the builder goes the other way.
